Ridge primitives, and three composition/loss variants compared

Motivation: a gaussian has zero gradient at its centre, so it can only make round hills -- its slope
distribution sits too low and its tail comes from where blobs superpose. Real terrain is closer to
uplift + incision + hillslope diffusion: long near-planar slopes, sharp crests, bounded steepness.

tools/ridge_model.py: landforms are segments (ridge lines) with a power-law profile
    h_i = A * clamp(1 - d_i/R, 0, 1)^gamma
        gamma = 1 -> cone (constant slope A/R)   gamma > 1 -> concave   gamma < 1 -> convex

Four variants measured against r.0.0 (512x512):

    variant                              rmse   corr   slope p50/p90/p99
    gaussian blobs (skeleton_model)       9.90  0.979   0.50 / 1.00 / --
    ridge, sum, pure L2                   9.34  0.982   0.64 / 1.04 / 1.31
    ridge, max composition, slope loss   18.92  0.922   0.71 / 1.50 / 2.50
    ridge, sum + slope limit (2.5)       10.75  0.975   0.71 / 1.12 / 2.00
    reference                              --     --    0.71 / 1.50 / 2.50

Conclusions from the renders (preview/images/ridge_compare.png, ridge_limited.png):
- ridge primitive fits better than gaussians (9.34 vs 9.90) -- the theory holds
- max composition is wrong: it only keeps the tallest landform at each point, which produces faceted
  cones (a low-poly look)
- matching the slope distribution by weighting the loss works numerically but costs layout accuracy
  (rmse 9.3 -> 18.9) and still looks faceted
- sum + an angle-of-repose limiter is the best balance: layout is kept (corr 0.98) and the slope tail
  is bounded by construction, giving large planar slopes with sharp creases

What is still missing: mid-scale roughness. Between the creases the surface is smooth, whereas the
reference has terraces and texture from 10 to 100 blocks. That is a detail-layer problem, not a
primitive problem.
